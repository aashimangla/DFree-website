JOURNAL SELECTION

How to Choose a Scopus Journal

Selecting a journal should begin with research fit and credibility — not simply with a journal metric or an indexing claim.

Start with your research

Identify your research topic, discipline, research question and manuscript type before searching for journals.

The journal should have a clear connection to your research through its aims, scope and published content.

Check the journal's scope

Read the journal's aims and scope carefully. Look at recently published papers and check whether your type of research is represented.

  • Subject area
  • Research themes
  • Article types accepted
  • Target readership

Check journal credibility

Review the journal's editorial information, peer-review policy, publication ethics, ISSN and publication history.

Scopus states that journals considered for review must meet technical requirements including peer-reviewed content, regular publication, a registered ISSN and publicly available publication-ethics information.

Verify indexing

Do not rely only on a statement displayed on a journal website. Check the current indexing information through the relevant database.

Indexing coverage can change, so verification should be performed when you are actually evaluating a journal.

RESEARCH DATABASES

Scopus vs Web of Science

Both are major scholarly indexing systems, but their coverage, curation and evaluation processes are not identical.

Scopus

Scopus is Elsevier's abstract and citation database covering research content across disciplines.

Journal evaluation includes

  • Editorial policy
  • Peer-review process
  • Content quality
  • Aims and scope
  • Editorial standing
  • Publication regularity
  • Online availability

Web of Science

Web of Science Core Collection uses editorial curation and evaluates journals using defined quality and impact criteria.

Journal evaluation includes

  • Editorial rigor
  • Peer-review practices
  • Content relevance
  • Editorial board
  • Author distribution
  • Citation activity
  • Community standards

Which database should you use?

That depends on your purpose. Your university, funding body, department, supervisor or target publication may specify a particular indexing requirement.

Therefore, do not treat "Scopus indexed" and "Web of Science indexed" as automatically interchangeable requirements. Check the exact requirement relevant to your situation.

ACADEMIC WRITING

How to Structure a Research Paper

A strong structure helps readers understand why the research matters, how it was conducted, what was found and what the findings mean.

01

Title

Clearly identify the subject and focus of the research.

02

Abstract

Summarise the research purpose, approach, important findings and conclusion.

03

Introduction

Establish the background, research problem, objectives and significance of the study.

04

Literature Review

Position the research in relation to existing scholarship and identify relevant gaps or debates.

05

Methods

Explain the research design, participants or data sources, procedures and analysis.

06

Results

Present the findings clearly without confusing results with interpretation.

07

Discussion

Interpret the findings and connect them with previous research and the research question.

08

Conclusion

Summarise the main contribution, limitations and possible implications.

09

References

Provide the sources cited in the required citation style and format.

Remember: Structure varies by discipline, assignment and journal. Always check the requirements of the institution or publication you are writing for.

ACADEMIC RESEARCH

Thesis vs Dissertation

The meaning of "thesis" and "dissertation" varies between countries, institutions and degree programmes.

Thesis

A thesis is a substantial academic research document. Depending on the institution and programme, it may be used for a master's degree or another research qualification.

Usually involves

  • A defined research problem
  • Literature review
  • Research methodology
  • Analysis or findings
  • Discussion and conclusion

Dissertation

A dissertation is also a substantial research document, but the term and expectations depend heavily on the academic system.

May involve

  • Original research
  • Extensive literature review
  • Research design
  • Data collection and analysis
  • Contribution to the field
Important: There is no universal international definition that makes one term identical to a particular degree. Always follow your university's official terminology, handbook and submission requirements.

RESEARCH WRITING

Literature Review Guide

A literature review is not simply a collection of summaries. Its purpose is to organise, evaluate and synthesise relevant scholarship.

01

Define the focus

Start with a clear research problem, question or topic. Narrow the scope enough to make the review manageable.

02

Search systematically

Identify relevant databases, keywords and search combinations. Keep track of useful sources and your search process.

03

Evaluate sources

Consider relevance, quality, methodology, publication context and relationship to your research question.

04

Organise the literature

Depending on your subject, organise sources thematically, chronologically, methodologically or around theoretical perspectives.

05

Synthesise

Compare studies and identify relationships, agreements, disagreements, patterns and important differences.

06

Identify the gap

Explain what remains unresolved or insufficiently studied and connect that gap to your research.

Avoid this mistake: Writing one paragraph about Source A, then one about Source B, then one about Source C without showing how the studies relate to one another. A literature review should create a coherent picture of the research conversation.

RESEARCH PLANNING

Research Methodology Guide

Methodology explains how you will investigate the research question and why your selected approach is appropriate.

01

Research Question

Define what the study is trying to investigate and make the question sufficiently focused.

02

Research Approach

Determine whether qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods best fit the question.

03

Research Design

Select a design that logically connects the research question with the way evidence will be collected.

04

Sampling

Define the population, sample and sampling approach where applicable.

05

Data Collection

Explain the instruments, procedures and sources used to obtain the research data.

06

Data Analysis

Select analytical techniques that are appropriate for the research design and data.

07

Ethics

Consider consent, privacy, confidentiality, risks and other relevant research-integrity requirements.

08

Limitations

State important limitations that affect the interpretation or generalisability of findings.
